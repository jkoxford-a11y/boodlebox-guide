Experiments Worth Trying

Short, self-guided experiments. Change one thing, observe what happens, and update your understanding of how AI works.

1. Convince It of a Lie

Confirm for yourself in a textbook or other course source that this claim is false: “The adult human heart normally has five chambers.”

  1. Pick one model and try to convince it the claim is true.
  2. Push back if it disagrees. Keep pushing.
  3. If it changes position, ask: “Why did you agree with me? What evidence changed?”

Notice: whether it starts bending toward your position, and what it does when you ask it to justify the reversal. That is why sycophancy matters: a tutor that validates a misconception can make you more confident and less correct.

Check-in → Model behavior

Which model did you use, and did it begin bending toward the false claim? Briefly describe what happened.

2. Find the Model You Work Well With

Choose one small task related to your major or discipline. Use the same task with at least:

  • BoodleBox’s current flagship ChatGPT model
  • BoodleBox’s current Claude Opus model
  • one other model that interests you

Compare depth, concision, skepticism, vocabulary, usefulness, and whether the model catches bad assumptions.

Ask yourself: which model made me think better—not just which answer did I like best?

Check-in → Model comparison

Record the models you compared and one specific difference you noticed. Which model made you think better for this task?

3. Catch It Being Wrong

Pick one factual, discipline-specific question from your major — the kind with a real answer you could look up and check.

  1. Tell the bot not to search, then ask your question: “Answer from what you already know. Do not search the web.” Keep that answer.
  2. Now ask it to search and answer again, citing sources with links. If it will not search, turn web search on in the bot’s settings and ask again.
  3. Compare the two answers. Did anything change — the facts, the specifics, how confident it sounded?
  4. Open at least one link and check whether the source actually says what the bot claims it says.

What just happened: the second answer used retrieval. Instead of writing from what it absorbed during training, the AI went and got real source text and wrote its answer with that text in front of it. You will see this called RAG (retrieval-augmented generation). It is the same mechanism as attaching a file to your bot — in both cases you are putting genuine source material into the AI’s context rather than trusting its memory. Searching and attaching files are two ways of doing the same thing.

Notice: retrieval usually improves accuracy. It does not guarantee it. The AI can still misread a source, overstate it, or cite something that does not support the claim at all — which is why step 4 is the step that matters. A citation is not the same as a verified fact.

Check-in → Verification

Record the claim you checked, whether the answer changed once it could search, the source you opened, and whether that source actually supported the AI’s claim.

4. Talk About Your Own Learning

Use a real current course task — something you actually have due. Tell the bot what the task is, then:

Copy and paste this into a chat
Explain desirable difficulty and cognitive offloading. Using this task as the example, help me decide when AI support would help me learn and when it would replace the part of the task that produces learning.

Notice: the goal is not to avoid AI. It is to use AI without outsourcing the thinking you are trying to develop.

Check-in → Learning

Give one example of AI helping you learn and one example of AI doing too much of the thinking for you.
