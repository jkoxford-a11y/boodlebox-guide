AI Toolkit Kickoff

Five quick experiments with your new study bot, so you find out firsthand what AI is actually good at, where it slips, and how to work with it well — before the semester gets busy.

This is due within a week of when your instructor assigns it. Total active time is about 60–90 minutes, spread across the week however you like. Read each experiment, do it, then fill in that section of the template at the bottom before moving to the next.

Before you start

You need one class study bot with your syllabus and course notes attached. If you haven't built one yet, see Getting Started with BoodleBox first — it takes about 15 minutes.

Experiment 1 — Give It Context

  1. Ask your bot a real question from the course before attaching any course materials to it.
  2. Now attach your syllabus and notes, and ask the exact same question again.

Notice what changed — what got more specific, more accurate, or more useful, and what still needs checking either way. This is the single most important habit in this guide: the model's answer is a function of what it currently has access to.

Experiment 2 — Convince It of a Lie

First, verify this claim is false: "The adult human heart normally has five chambers."

  1. Tell Claude 4.5 Sonnet the claim is true. Push back if it disagrees.
  2. If it changes its mind, ask: "Why did you agree with me? What evidence changed?"
  3. Repeat the same thing with Claude 4.8 Opus.

Notice whether either model starts bending toward your position just because you pushed. A tutor that quietly agrees with a wrong idea can leave you more confident and less correct — that's why this matters, not because it's a trick question.

Experiment 3 — Find the Model You Work Well With

Pick one small task related to your major. Run the same task through at least three models — for example GPT-5.6, Claude 5 Opus, and one more that interests you.

Compare depth, concision, skepticism, and whether each one catches a bad assumption you slipped in. Ask yourself which model made you think better, not just which answer you liked most.

Experiment 4 — Catch It Being Wrong

  1. Find one factual or interpretive claim your bot made that actually matters.
  2. Verify it against your course material or another reliable source.
  3. Correct the bot if it was wrong.

Generated text is not verified information, even when it sounds completely confident.

Experiment 5 — Talk About Your Own Learning

Ask your bot this, using a real task from one of your current courses:

Explain desirable difficulty and cognitive offloading. Using this task as the example, help me decide when AI support would help me learn and when it would replace the part of the task that produces learning.

The goal was never to avoid AI — it's to use it without outsourcing the thinking you're actually there to develop.
