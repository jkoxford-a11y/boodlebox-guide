Using AI Well

Four ideas explain most of the successes and failures you will see when working with AI.

1Context matters

Context is the information AI has available when it responds. That can include what you type in the current conversation, instructions and knowledge attached to a custom bot, and information the AI retrieves with tools or search.

If the information AI needs is not in its context, the answer may be generic, based on a bad assumption, or simply wrong.

What it looks like
Thin context“Help me study for my psychology exam.” The bot does not know your course, what your instructor emphasized, or what is on the exam.
Useful contextYour class bot has the syllabus, your course-notes ledger, study materials, and tutor instructions. You ask it to quiz you on the material covered since the last exam.
Habit: when the answer misses the mark, ask first: What information is the AI missing?

2Fluent does not mean correct

AI can confidently generate incorrect facts, fabricated details, bad summaries, or poor advice. This can happen in a short chat or a long one. Confidence and polished writing are not evidence.

Long or overloaded chats add another problem: the AI can miss, blur, mis-weight, or contradict information that appeared earlier.

When is a chat getting long?

Practical rule of thumb, not a technical limit: around 30 substantive back-and-forth messages is a good warning point to consider a fresh chat. Start sooner if you pasted a lot of material, changed topics repeatedly, or notice the AI forgetting instructions or contradicting earlier work.

Ask the AI to create a handoff, then continue in a fresh conversation with the same class bot. The setup page gives you a handoff prompt.

3AI adapts to you

Sycophancy is when AI bends toward what you appear to want or believe rather than maintaining an independently justified answer.

This matters because agreement can feel like evidence. A tutor that politely confirms a misconception can leave you more confident and less correct. Sycophancy can reinforce bad interpretations, produce poor advice, and damage learning.

What it looks like
Naive“Negative reinforcement basically means punishment, right?” The student treats agreement as confirmation.
Competent“Evaluate this claim: negative reinforcement means punishment. Give the strongest reason it could be wrong.” The student asks for evaluation rather than validation.
Habit: if the AI reverses itself after you push back, ask: What new evidence changed your answer?

4Models differ

Different models can vary in depth, speed, skepticism, verbosity, vocabulary, and reliability. There is no single best model for every task.

As a working heuristic, smaller and faster models are useful for simple work, but speed often comes with reduced capability. For difficult reasoning or information where mistakes matter, switch to a stronger model and still verify the result.

What it looks like
NaiveUses the default model for every task and assumes all models will behave about the same.
CompetentUses a fast model for a simple formatting task, but switches to a stronger model for difficult reasoning and checks important claims either way.
